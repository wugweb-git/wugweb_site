Navigating the complexities of sustainability on the web

We explore the tensions between creative expression, meaningful measurement, and honest communication in building a more sustainable web.

Written by Kenny Heard, Engineer
08 March 2026

The digital world that surrounds us today often feels weightless. It's a space of screens, pixels, and clicks. Websites in particular are often our gateway into this space, and whilst they don't leave physical marks like tyre tracks or smoke trails, they do leave a footprint.

Every visit to every website is powered by real infrastructure consuming real resources, and as the web continues to grow, so does its demand on the planet.

Sustainability is rarely framed around the web, and yet it's a tool we all use to build and consume content daily. Thankfully, this is starting to change within the creative industry. The question has become less about how the web affects the environment and more about how we respond to this reality. That response, however, is not a simple one to craft. Every decision for a given project, from conception to delivery, creates opportunities for tension.

The Core Tensions

As the platforms and services we use day-to-day evolve, so do our expectations of what they can deliver. Today's web experiences are expected to do everything — inspire, delight, perform, convert — all whilst minimising environmental impact. The challenge is that these goals often directly conflict with each other. Understanding these tensions is essential for making informed decisions that balance creativity, performance, and responsibility.

At Driftime, we've dedicated a lot of our time and energy to uncovering and alleviating these tensions, and we've identified three prominent ones:

Expression vs. Efficiency

Have you ever come across a website that's blown you away? Today's web is capable of supporting extremely rich and vibrant experiences with striking visuals, high-quality media, and smooth motion. Modern developer tooling and assistance from the ever-growing intelligence of LLMs has made these experiences easier than ever to achieve, but at what cost?

These heavier web experiences often rely on larger file sizes for media assets and the libraries powering much of the experience. More data means more energy, from the servers hosting the content to the devices using it. It's a direct relationship that's easy to overlook when we're focused on the visual impact.

Data provided by HTTP Archive's Web Almanac shows the median desktop home page weight rose from roughly 1.2 MB in 2014 to 2.86 MB by July 2025, and mobile from around 0.5 MB to 2.56 MB — a year-on-year increase of 7.3% on desktop and 8.4% on mobile. This reflects a paradox: better tools, smarter caching and static site generation should lead to leaner experiences, but instead they've made it easier to justify heavier ones.

Here's an important distinction though: expression isn't inherently wasteful, nor is it a bad thing. Naturally, as a creative agency, expression is at the core of our process. For many industries, a visually immersive presence isn't just decoration — it's part of the product. In these contexts, heavier assets can be justified if they serve a clear purpose and are delivered as responsibly as possible.

The real challenge isn't stripping the web entirely of expression, but being intentional — questioning every animation, every autoplaying video, and every giant image, and asking whether they are truly adding value or simply adding weight.

Metrics vs. Meaning

So if expression comes at a cost, how do we actually measure it? Carbon calculators and performance analysers each provide a way to quantify how much energy a website might consume or how it performs. These tools can act as a guide towards building more thoughtful, sustainable web experiences.

However, they can only ever tell part of the story. Most work on simplified assumptions, and whilst they can provide useful benchmarks, they fall short when you introduce modern practices like serverless architectures, CDNs, or caching. At Driftime, we host our projects on Vercel. Their edge networks and fluid compute model distribute content closer to users and scale server resources based on demand — powerful for both performance and energy usage. But these practices carry no weight when fed to the aforementioned tools.

Vercel runs on Amazon Web Services, which as of 2024 reports being powered by 100% renewable energy, independently verified by a third party. It's worth noting that "100% renewable" at hyperscaler scale is often achieved through renewable energy certificates and power purchase agreements rather than direct supply to every data centre, so the picture is more nuanced than a single claim suggests. Still, it represents a meaningful commitment.

So does this mean we can demonstrate to clients that their websites run on cutting-edge infrastructure and are completely green? Not quite. The way modern web infrastructure works creates verification challenges that carbon calculators and automated checkers weren't designed to handle, and this is where sustainability measurement starts to become a question of trust.

Trust vs. Transparency

If the numbers don't always tell the full story, the question becomes: how do we communicate sustainability in a way that's transparent, trustworthy, and meaningful?

Many websites today, including our own, are built as distributed systems rather than running on one central server. This distribution is beneficial — content loads faster and more reliably — but it makes sustainability measurement much trickier. If your site is cached on a server in Frankfurt, does that mean it's powered by Germany's energy grid? What happens if the request falls back to a data centre in Virginia?

Carbon calculators typically work by looking at a hosting provider's IP address, finding the associated data centre, and making assumptions about its energy source. When your site could be served from anywhere across a distributed network, that direct lookup becomes much harder to achieve. At Driftime, we've long struggled with the irony that our websites are reported as "not green" by automated checkers, even though we know they're running on infrastructure powered by renewable energy.

This is not just a technical problem — it's a trust problem. One where transparency becomes as important as the numbers themselves.

We reached out to both Vercel and the Green Web Foundation to help us navigate this. Vercel confirmed that their infrastructure runs on AWS, but stated that they could not share their IP ranges with Green Web Foundation, making automated verification impossible. However, by providing our own evidence that our site is hosted on Vercel, that Vercel runs on AWS, and that AWS is powered by renewable energy, we were able to have driftime.com verified. 🎉

Despite these verification challenges, we chose Vercel as our primary hosting platform. The developer experience is smooth and, as the team behind Next.js, it offers the most natural integration for our tech stack.

Looking ahead

Building sustainable websites isn't about choosing one ideal path. It's about navigating tensions and making deliberate choices. Every project involves balancing expression and efficiency, weighing metrics against meaning, and aligning trust with transparency.

At Driftime, our approach isn't to chase perfection — it's to be intentional. Every design choice and technical decision should have a clear rationale, because expression has value only when it serves the experience. Tools like carbon calculators and PageSpeed Insights provide guidance, not authority, and understanding their limitations matters as much as the scores they produce.

What's considered sustainable for one project may not suit another. Sustainability on the web isn't a trade-off between creativity and responsibility. It's a practice of thoughtful choices, clear communication, and conscious design — creating web experiences that delight people, serve brands, and respect the planet, all at once.
